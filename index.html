How much depends on our emissions

    Write-up

    Goal

    Climate projections are usually reported as one global number: "+2°C", "+4°C". Those numbers are hard to relate to everyday life. We wanted readers to look at the place they live and ask what the projections mean there. So the page is a short story. It opens on a provocative question, shows the whole country under a worst-case future, lets the reader pick their own state, and then turns that state's numbers into plain language: how much hotter, how much wetter or drier, and how much of that depends on our choices about emissions.

    Data

    All climate values come from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). We accessed CMIP6 through the public Pangeo/Google Cloud archive (gs://cmip6), using the catalog in data.csv to find the right Zarr stores. We used one model, IPSL-CM6A-LR (ensemble member r1i1p1f1), and two monthly variables from table Amon:

    • tas: near-surface air temperature, converted from kelvin to °C.
    • pr: precipitation flux (rain plus snow), converted from kg m-2 s-1 to mm/day by multiplying by 86,400.

    We pulled the historical experiment for the 1995–2014 baseline, the reference period used by the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report. We pulled four ScenarioMIP experiments for 2015–2100:

    • SSP1-2.6 (Low emissions): emissions fall sharply, keeping global warming near 2°C.
    • SSP2-4.5 (Moderate emissions): a middle-of-the-road path that continues current trends with modest climate policy.
    • SSP3-7.0 (High emissions): regional rivalry, high energy demand and little decarbonization.
    • SSP5-8.5 (Very high emissions): fossil-fueled development, the upper end of the projections.

    Processing

    The script main.py does the processing:

    1. Converts longitudes from 0–360° to −180–180° and cuts the global grid down to a box around the United States.
    2. Averages the monthly values into annual means.
    3. Clips each year's grid to every state's boundary (U.S. Census 2018 cartographic boundaries) and averages the grid cells inside it. We use all_touched so that small states still pick up grid cells. DC is smaller than one grid cell, so it falls back to the nearest cell to its centroid.
    4. Writes one record per state to state_projections.json: the 1995–2014 baseline and the annual 2015–2100 series for each scenario.

    The browser then shows every value as a change from the 1995–2014 baseline. Temperature is shown in degrees of warming and precipitation as a percent change. A single model year is mostly weather rather than climate. Year-to-year rainfall in one state can swing by ±30%. So every number on the page is an average over an 11-year window centred on the chosen year. At the end of the record the window is cut short, so "2100" means the 2095–2100 average.

    Design decisions

    • Scrolling story first, exploration second. Many readers won't touch a control. So the default view already makes the main point: the whole country under SSP5-8.5 in 2100. Interaction then personalizes that point instead of being needed to find it.
    • Choropleth by state. States are the geographic unit readers recognize fastest and can find "their" place in. The model grid is about 2.5° × 1.3°, so finer units like counties or cities would claim more precision than the data has.
    • Color. Warming is positive everywhere in every scenario, so temperature uses a single-hue sequential scale (light orange to dark red, 0–10°C). Rainfall can go either way, so it uses a diverging brown-to-teal scale centred on 0%. Brown means drier, teal means wetter, and an unchanged state stays neutral.
    • Selecting a state. Readers click a state instead of picking it from a list. This keeps their eyes on the map and shows their state in context with its neighbours.
    • Plain-language impacts. The impacts step gives temperatures in °F and rainfall in inches per year because our audience is American. It puts the baseline next to the projection so the change has a reference point.
    • Comparing scenarios. The impacts step ends by listing all four emissions scenarios side by side for the same state. This shows that the future isn't fixed: the gap between SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 is often larger than the low-emissions change itself.
    • Pan and zoom. Dragging pans the map. The scroll wheel only zooms while Ctrl/⌘ is held, so the map doesn't hijack page scrolling in a scroll-snapping story.
    • Tooltips give the exact value and the baseline period, for readers who want a number rather than a color.

    Limitations

    • We use a single model and a single ensemble member. Other CMIP6 models disagree, especially on rainfall, so these are one plausible outcome, not a forecast.
    • The grid is coarse. Small states such as Rhode Island and Delaware are covered by only one or two grid cells, and grid cells are averaged without area weighting.
    • Annual means hide changes in seasons and extremes, such as heat waves, droughts and heavy downpours, which matter most for daily life.
    • The western Aleutian Islands lie beyond 180° longitude and fall outside the processing box.
